Abstract

The objective of this work was to study learning techniques—specifically supervised learning—for the prediction of non-existent or hidden links between users within the same social network.

Networks could be represented as graphs, in which the nodes corresponded to users and the edges to the relationships between them. From the perspective of supervised learning, it was necessary to create datasets containing all the information from the graphs. The instances of these datasets corresponded to each pair of nodes, and the attributes to the similarity values between them, based on various functions. The class of the instances was determined by the presence or absence of a link between the nodes; therefore, link prediction became a binary classification problem.

The Gephi tool was used as a foundation for implementing the necessary requirements to conduct the experiments. It provided utilities for graph manipulation and for calculating various similarity functions between pairs of nodes. Additionally, new functionalities were added for dataset creation and classification from graphs, as well as a set of techniques to enhance and make the classification process more flexible: instance filtering, feature selection, and dataset balancing.

Four networks with very different characteristics were used for the experiments, varying in both size and intrinsic structure. Three of the networks ranged from small to medium in size, while the fourth and most important one corresponded to a crawl of a portion of Twitter and was therefore significantly larger. It was also the most disconnected network of the four.

Due to the vast number of possible combinations of networks, techniques, and classifiers to be tested, the experimentation was carried out in stages. Since individual similarity functions were useful on their own for link prediction, though with very different behaviors, the objective of the final stage was to compare the supervised learning approach against these functions, evaluating the quality of both types of predictive models.

Various conclusions were reached. Instance filtering based on common neighbors improved classification in all cases. Feature selection proved beneficial for small and medium-sized networks. Dataset balancing was particularly useful for the Twitter dataset, where extreme imbalance had previously prevented significantly meaningful classifications. Finally, the learning-based approach provided greater quality and flexibility than individual functions; however, it required more extensive testing and configuration. In large networks, function-based approaches became ineffective due to their low flexibility and performance.
